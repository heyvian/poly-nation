# Poly Nation / Infinite Rinse

ARCH 606 · SAPL · University of Calgary · November 2026

## Architecture as a cross-pollination system.

Poly Nation pairs a permanent civic ring with removable rooms at its edge. At the former Canada Creosote site in West Village, Calgary, Infinite Rinse makes repair part of the public experience. The project tests whether programme, routes and shared spaces can create more opportunities for people and other occupants to meet.

## Repair as a public project.

Poly Nation places civic life above a ground in repair. Infinite Rinse brings pumping, filtration, cultivation and public education into the architectural programme.

### Ground and water

The studio project addresses the former Canada Creosote site in Calgary’s West Village. Its drawings propose contained groundwater circulation, treatment and controlled floodwater storage. The greenhouse makes parts of that work visible from a separate public route.

### The public proposition

Putting care, recreation and a working remediation landscape in close proximity asks who gets to spend time on a site in transition. The river, the treatment system and the civic building operate at different scales and on different time horizons.

### Ecological stakes

The studio proposal distinguishes treatment water from the public recreation circuit. The building model studies visibility, circulation and occupation. Contaminant containment, flood behaviour, treatment capacity and water quality require their own site-specific evidence.

## Atrium and roofline

The centre joins public circulation, a visible water landscape and a taller roof silhouette.

### Opposing lobby atriums

Two ground-floor lobbies face each other on an axis perpendicular to the lift cores. Straight paths lead from the plaza through the biofiltration greenhouse and join a ring around the swing mast. The designer proposed the crossing to create more opportunities for indoor and outdoor visitors to meet. Upper galleries cross both lobby voids.

### Radial and vertically aligned bridges

Optional bridges pass close to the mast and connect the upper corridors. They let the designer compare whether shorter routes change encounter patterns. Stacking the bridges keeps the crossings on one axis. A radial arrangement changes the view and orientation of each crossing. Guards line the bridges and the open atrium edges.

### Pitched glass roof and two-storey pavilions

The designer raised the greenhouse ridge by up to about two storeys and used occasional second storeys on the rooftop shops to vary the skyline. The swing boarding platform completes the glazed roof at the mast. Both storeys of an enclosed shop count toward the area cap.

## A stable core. A changing periphery.

The ring gives long-lived services, shared space and circulation a permanent address. The shorter-lived rooms attach to its edge as removable units.

### The permanent ring

The designer put a mixed programme in the annulus and gave the full-height centre to the Infinite Rinse greenhouse and biofilter. Putting rooms in the ring creates a lived-in core with its own places to meet.

### A repeatable bay

A single module is 4.5 × 18 × 3.5 m in the study, with 81 m² of floor area. The original studio scheme also considered paired units. A standard bay lets occupied modules, small shops and public outdoor decks use a common attachment system.

## One programme budget, two modes of occupation.

The split between core rooms and plug-in modules is a design choice for each of the five programmes.

### Targets and built area

The 20,000 m² baseline includes Infinite Rinse, Love Hotel, Marine Firehall, Daycare and Senior Care. The targets guide the distribution. The actual areas depend on whole modules, usable room sizes and space reserved for access.

### What enters the budget

The greenhouse floor counts once beneath the full-height void. Enclosed retail, rooftop shop storeys, lift and service rooms, lobbies and circulation take up area. The plaza, outdoor gardens and open balconies sit outside the enclosed total. The 80 m² default minimum describes schematic programme zones.

## Relationships are the design input.

The Topology Designer lets the designer specify which programmes should draw together, keep apart or share the building without a preference.

### Attract, repel and neutral

Node distance sets the strength of a pair’s relationship. Attraction favours nearby rooms and docks and can generate shared gathering space. Repulsion prioritizes separation on the same floor, while vertical neighbours and shared public routes are still possible. All pairs start neutral.

### Clustering and priority

The designer can cluster any programme. A selected programme groups even at minimum clustering strength, and raising the strength reinforces its tendency to group. Pair relationships still inform the layout at every clustering setting.

### Marine Firehall

Firehall modules fill eligible ground-floor bays first. Each successive floor can carry at most as many as the level immediately below. Clustering can then concentrate them around one part of the ring.

*Figure. Example relationship settings, not the default. By default all inter-program relationships are neutral and explicit clustering is off.*

## Attraction becomes a place to gather.

The corridor, lobbies and shortcuts shape who passes through, who stops and who can see into another part of the building.

### Two kinds of threshold

Rooms with an attract relationship can share a furnished lobby when there is space to make one. Separate thresholds for repelling programmes still connect to the common route. The outer corridor reaches each active dock, and two opposing lift cores connect the levels.

### A local bypass

The slide needs direct connections into two Infinite Rinse rooms. The designer moved those rooms against the opaque façade and took the corridor behind them. The same move lets eligible rooms reach the façade where the slide occupies neighbouring docking space.

## The public route wraps the building.

The public realm runs around the building, through its greenhouse and over its roof.

### A continuous ground loop

The designer expanded the entrance plaza into a promenade connecting the two lobbies and the climbing approach. Patios, seating, planting, sports and gathering areas occupy islands along the loop. Nearby programmes inform the activity mix.

### A public roof

Both lifts reach the annular roof park. Paths, gardens and places to sit share the deck with independently placed shops. Empty bays can extend public life outward as balconies and gardens, alongside private module balconies.

## Play changes the shape of the building.

The designer uses the swing, slide and climb to draw people into different paths through the building.

### A swing through the atrium

The Prater-inspired ride puts the mast at the centre of occupied space. Its boarding platform sits at the ridge of the greenhouse roof. Stairs rise over the glazing, and the moving crown is drawn with tapered radial trusses inspired by the designer’s section sketch.

### A slide with two rooms

The slide leaves an upper Infinite Rinse room from a stepped boarding platform. The tube has bolted sections and fitted sleeves at both façade crossings. The lower room receives the tube into a longer pool aligned to its direction of travel. The basin is drawn to fit the room.

### Climb and return

Climbers start and finish at the public promenade. The slide bows outward at a crossing with the climbing wall, and the affected docking space is reserved. The study represents a clearance strategy. Ride operations, separation from process water and pool hydraulics require specialist design.

*Figure. Ride, slide and climb are linked to their surroundings, not placed independently as objects. Schematic only.*

*Figure. The basin follows the tube’s exit heading and extends into the receiving room. Its length adjusts with the room geometry. The diagram describes the architectural relationship.*

## Who uses the shared route?

The encounters study includes human visitors and more-than-human occupants with different movement patterns.

### Different ways to occupy

People arrive, visit rooms and modules, use lifts, linger in shared spaces and seek out play. Dogs stay on public routes, while birds, squirrels and rabbits use outdoor destinations. The study includes wheelchair users and a range of body sizes as part of the designer’s question about who the building serves.

### Leaving a room

What happens after someone leaves a room matters to the experiment. The model remembers the last room and sends some visitors briefly toward a shared lobby. Movement, time spent in rooms and attraction to play are assumed behaviours. Accessibility and actual use remain separate questions.

*Figure. Conceptual state diagram. Agents move, dwell, remember, meet and move on. Paths and timings are model assumptions, not observed human or animal behavior.*

## Measure opportunities to meet.

The designer asks whether different arrangements create more occasions for people and other occupants to cross paths.

### Context matters

The model counts proximity encounters within eligible shared spaces. It weights a shared lobby encounter more heavily than a pass in a corridor. Recent rooms, recent programmes, different agent kinds and recent play can affect a pair’s score. The rate is expressed per 100 active visitor-minutes.

### Reading the result

The weights and timing thresholds are modelling assumptions. The score measures opportunities for co-presence in the simulation. The useful comparison asks which paths and destinations brought occupants together, and whether a pattern repeats across comparable trials.

*Figure. Default weights in points per ten credited seconds. The student directed the ordering. The numerical weights are modelling assumptions.*

## Compare architectures as hypotheses.

Keep the programme budget in view while testing what placement, circulation and the permanent-to-modular split do to encounters.

### Layouts and allocations

One search samples alternative dock placements with the rooms, programme areas and module inventory fixed. A separate search exchanges whole modules and the same area of core space between programmes, keeping each programme’s total area fixed. Both retain the topology and placement constraints.

### Matched trials

Candidates use the same sets of five-minute simulated trials with matched populations and weights. The searches compare sampled alternatives. In review, the score can guide a closer look at floor plans, routes and who meets where. Repeated runs across seeds help distinguish a pattern from one favourable variation.

*Figure. Feedback loop: modify design parameters, generate a building, simulate occupation, observe the score, then reconsider the architecture.*

## Where programs meet the ring.

Permanent rooms and removable modules occupy different parts of the same circulation network.

### Placement as a local decision

The designer can move a programme into an empty eligible dock or reassign a room or a module. The model checks the area budget, same-floor repulsion, firehall stacking, access and reserved bays so a local decision remains legible at building scale.

### Balconies and laundry

Semicircular balconies attach to the module tips for Senior Care, Love Hotel, upper-floor Firehall and a sample of Infinite Rinse. Some Senior Care modules share laundry lines with the neighbouring enclosure. These details distinguish different kinds of occupation in a repeated structural bay.

### Vacancy as capacity

An available bay can stay ready for a future module or carry a public balcony, garden deck or enclosed shop. The public decks are removable assemblies carried by the permanent support. Private tip balconies belong to their modules.

*Figure. Generated ground-floor plan (seed 606) with Firehall clustering and Love Hotel–Daycare Repel. The five red Firehall modules occupy neighboring docks near the red Firehall room. The lower-left gap in the modules leaves room for the entry approach.*

## What stays when a module leaves?

The permanent ring and dock structure retain capacity for change. A module can leave while the core and neighbouring bays continue to have an enclosure and a load path.

### A dock between the ring and the module

The designer sketched a two-leaf closure that drops to seal an empty bay and folds overhead when the threshold is in use. Permanent support continues past vacant bays and carries gardens and balconies. The dock frame stops where the slide, entrances and climbing wall need space. Columns sit between bays, and radial members follow the dock grid.

### A wall face and a load path

The baseline model uses paired tapered side trusses. The designer questioned how their depth and diagonals compete with windows. The study compares open-panel side frames, a shallow box floor, same-storey cable stays, roof-hung rods and outer posts. Deep underfloor options were removed after they occupied the bays below.

### The same-storey stay

The designer brought each cable anchor down to the top of its own storey. The shallow angle gives a slimmer side elevation and a larger horizontal reaction. A bolted end-plate splice aligns the stay with a radial steel collector that returns into the core. The joint and inboard transfer require engineering design. The roof-hung study returns the upper reaction into a collector below the public deck so the rooftop shops and routes stay clear. The moment joint and inboard tie need design. Tip posts shift support to the plaza edge.

### A scale check

The dock projects 18.65 m in the model. In the reference gravity study a fitted-out enclosed module is about 20.5 t before people and snow. With the assumptions used for the default model, a saturated garden produces about 7.6 MN·m at the core face as a free cantilever. The same-storey stay study gives a tension near 1 MN per cable. The 220, 260 and 300 mm slab variants explore floor depth around roughly 2.7 m radial-beam spacing at the default rim. These are schematic order-of-magnitude checks for comparing load paths.

*Figure. Both options work within one storey in the schematic section. The cable opens up the side elevation and shifts demand into its anchors and deck. The diagrams compare load paths.*

## Give the frame and the enclosure different lives.

The designer separates the permanent load-bearing system from finishes and the parts expected to be removed or exchanged.

### Core and outer leaf

The designer proposed a steel frame capable of taking dock reactions, with a separately supported rammed-earth outer leaf. The earth gives the permanent core a different weight and texture from its lightweight plug-ins. Its source, testing, moisture detailing and support remain design questions, especially on a contaminated site.

### Lightweight plug-ins and winter shops

Steel, lightweight metal and engineered timber assemblies are material-study options for the modules. The rooftop shops are independently enclosed prefabricated units on skids, with straight sides, insulated panels and glazed storefronts. Some stack a set-back second unit. Their construction and conditioning are shown schematically.

### Programme and finish

The designer wanted a recognizably different daycare edge. The material study varies bright glossy paint across those modules. It also tests finish and sheen on the slide. Related cladding tones make individual modules legible at building scale. The repeated frame and dock dimensions keep them part of the same system.

### Compare quantities

The quantity study compares rough Canadian-dollar cost and assembly mass within its stated rate assumptions. The exploded views let a jury read frame, enclosure and finishes as separate decisions. Fabrication, maintenance, site remediation and actual procurement need their own estimates.

## Design direction and scope.

The architecture and the computational study have distinct roles in the jury evidence.

### Designer’s direction

The student developed the site proposition, programme mix, ring and plug-in system, relationships, routes, recreational architecture and material questions. Iterations include the two lobby crossings, bridge arrangements, roof profile, docking closure, same-storey stays, removable rooftop shops and painted daycare.

### Model development

The assistant translated the decisions into generated geometry, placement rules, a circulation graph, study drawings and estimates. Numerical thresholds, trial weights, schematic member sizes and some details were proposed in that work and are presented as assumptions. The record draws on the studio package, the available design conversation and the versioned model.

### Questions for review

How visible should treatment and maintenance be from the public route? Which connection allows a module to leave without undermining the weather enclosure or everyday use of the building? Which adjacency or route genuinely supports different occupants meeting? The plans, sections, assembly comparisons and matched trials offer different kinds of evidence for these questions.
